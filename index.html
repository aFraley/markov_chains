Markov Chains and Their Evolution

One assumption: the next symbol depends only on the last few. From Markov counting letters in Pushkin by hand, through Shannon's approximations of English, to the n-gram language models that ran speech recognition and machine translation for decades.

1913 Markov → 1948 Shannon → Order-1 → Order-N + backoff → Smoothing → The wall
- - -
1906 · 1913

A. A. Markov: dependence you can count

In 1906 Markov introduced chains of dependent events to show that the law of large numbers does not require independence. In 1913 he put the idea to work on real text: he took the first 20,000 letters of Pushkin's Eugene Onegin, classified each as vowel or consonant, and counted what followed what.

Current letterP(next is vowel)P(next is consonant)
vowel0.1280.872
consonant0.6630.337
What it shows: If letters were independent, both rows would be identical (vowels make up about 43% of the text). They are wildly different. The current state carries real information about the next one, and a two-row table captures it.
1948

Claude Shannon: Markov chains as models of language

In A Mathematical Theory of Communication, Shannon generated a series of "approximations to English": random letters, then letters drawn with the right frequencies, then letters conditioned on the previous one, then on the previous two, then the same trick at the word level. Each step reads more like English. He was doing what this project does, with a book and a table of random numbers instead of a computer.

Shannon's ladder
order-0 P(c) # letter frequencies only order-1 P(c | c₋₁) # digrams: Markov's table, all 26 letters order-2 P(c | c₋₂ c₋₁) # trigrams order-n P(c | c₋ₙ ... c₋₁) # n-grams
What it adds: A Markov chain is not just a way to describe text, it is a way to generate it, and the order is a dial between gibberish and plausibility. Shannon also used these models to estimate the entropy of English, which is where "language model" as a measurable thing begins.
Step 1 · this repo

Order-1 character chain name_generator.py

Markov's table with all the letters filled in. Count how often each letter follows each other letter in a set of mythology names, then walk the table: pick a start letter, sample the next from its row, repeat. Name length and first letter get their own distributions.

The whole algorithm
P(next | current) = count(current → next) / count(current → anything)
One knob: power scaling
weight = floor(count ** 1.3) # >1 sharpens, <1 flattens (a temperature)
The limitation: after generating "th", the chain sees only "h". Output has the right letters in plausible pairs and little else.
Step 2 · this repo

Order-N with backoff name_generator_higher_order.py

Use the last N characters as the state. Quality jumps immediately, and two new problems show up at once. Most long contexts never occur in the training data, so the chain needs a fallback. And the contexts that do occur are so specific that the chain starts reciting its training set.

Backoff: if the long context was never seen, shorten it
for o in range(order, 0, -1): context = name[-o:] if context in chain: return sample(chain[context])
The limitation: there are 26N possible contexts and a few hundred training names. Raise N and you trade gibberish for memorization. Try it below: the copy rate climbs with every order.

1987 – 2007

Smoothing: the n-gram era

Step 2's backoff is the crude version. Once n-gram models became the engine of speech recognition and statistical machine translation, the field spent two decades on one question: how much probability should an unseen context get, and where should it come from? (Discussed here for context; not implemented in this repo.)

MethodYearIdea
Add-one (Laplace)1700sPretend every event was seen once more than it was. Simple, and badly overestimates the unseen.
Good-Turing1953Estimate the mass of unseen events from how many events were seen exactly once.
Katz backoff1987Discount seen counts, hand the saved mass to the shorter context. Our backoff, made into a real probability distribution.
Kneser-Ney1995When backing off, ask how many different contexts a symbol follows, not how often it appears. The long-standing best n-gram smoother.
Stupid backoff2007Skip normalization, just multiply by 0.4 and back off. At web scale, with trillions of words, it was good enough.
What it teaches: the hard part of an n-gram model is never the counting, it is deciding what to believe about everything you didn't count.
The wall

A fixed window is a hard ceiling

Every model on this page shares one property: nothing older than the last N symbols can influence the next one. Making N larger is the only way to remember more, and each step multiplies the number of contexts while the data stays the same size. Smoothing softens the sparsity; it can't touch the window.

The next step was to stop storing contexts and start learning a representation of them (Bengio et al., 2003), then to drop the window entirely: RNNs, then LSTMs, then transformers. That is a different story, and this page stops here.

Other branches of the same idea

Language was only one direction the Markov property went.

  • Markov chain Monte Carlo (1953). Metropolis and colleagues built a chain whose long-run distribution is the one you want to sample from. Walk it long enough and you are sampling it. The basis of modern Bayesian computation.
  • Hidden Markov models (late 1960s–1980s). The states are hidden and only emit observations. Baum and colleagues gave the training algorithm; at IBM and elsewhere, HMMs paired with n-gram language models became the standard architecture for speech recognition.
  • PageRank (1998). A random surfer clicking links is a Markov chain over web pages; a page's rank is how often the surfer is there in the long run.
